# Research memo: Is the Decision Tree solid business practice?

Prepared 2026-10-27 by a research pass over established decision-rights and delegation literature. Paraphrased throughout; sources at the end.

**Bottom line:** Yes. Every established framework checked agrees on the core premise: routine, reversible, narrow decisions belong at the front line; cross-functional and high-stakes decisions escalate; identity, ownership, and leadership-team people decisions stay with the owner. The tree is a plain-language version of what Bain, McKinsey, HBR and the second-stage research all say. The attribution to Susan Scott's *Fierce Conversations* is correct and already handled in Shannon's copy (inspired by, grown since).

**A note on framing.** The research brief described the model as four levels sorted by "altitude." Shannon's actual model is five levels sorted by what the decider owes the owner (decide / decide then tell / talk first / together / owner with input). The findings below still apply; where the memo says "altitude," read it as the practical effect of those five rules.

## 1. What the established frameworks say

**RAPID (Bain; Rogers & Blenko, HBR 2006, "Who Has the D?").** Five roles per decision, exactly one person holds the D. Their survey of 350+ organizations found only about 15% practiced effective decision making; bottlenecks are structural. RAPID assigns roles; it does not classify decisions by weight. The Decision Tree fills that gap.

**McKinsey decision-type taxonomy (2018, 1,228 executives).** Big bets (rare, high stakes), cross-cutting (frequent, multi-group), delegated (frequent, narrow). Respondents spend roughly 37% of their time on decisions and 61% say most of it is spent ineffectively. Organizations that push delegated decisions to the right level were 6.8x more likely to be top performers. Maps nearly one-to-one: big bets = root/trunk, cross-cutting = trunk/limb, delegated = leaf/branch.

**"Who's Got the Monkey?" (Oncken & Wass, HBR 1974).** The classic on how subordinates' problems migrate onto the manager's back. The Decision Tree Check is, in effect, a monkey count.

**Levels of delegation.** Hyatt's five levels and Management 3.0's seven (Delegation Poker, recorded on a board everyone can see) treat delegation as a dial, not a switch. Supports a result that says "hand this branch back first," not "delegate everything." The delegation board is a close cousin of the tree-on-the-wall story.

**EOS / Traction, Delegate and Elevate.** Owner sorts tasks into four quadrants and delegates from the bottom up. Practitioner parallel; EOS publishes no target time split.

**Scaling Up (Harnish).** Founders' inability to release functions they once ran is treated as the first barrier to scaling. One owner per accountability.

**Greiner's growth stages (HBR 1972/1998).** The crisis of leadership (founder can't run everything informally) is resolved by structure; the crisis of autonomy is resolved by delegation, which is often the hardest change for the founder. Academic backbone for "the owner becomes the bottleneck."

**Edward Lowe Foundation, second stage.** Second stage is roughly 10–99 employees, $1M–$50M, nearly Syncovate's audience. Frames the owner's job as moving from technician to coach, and names "identify what to delegate, hire managers, trust them" as the central challenge. Shannon's own Lowe background makes this a natural citation.

**Wasserman, founder's dilemmas.** Founders who hold control tend to cap growth. VC-startup data; cite the principle, not the stats.

**Collins and Watkins** are thin on this question. "First who, then what" supports treating leadership-team people decisions as root level. Not worth leaning on.

## 2. Evidence on owner time and the cost of not delegating

- **Gallup (2015), Inc. 500 CEOs (n=143):** CEOs with high "Delegator" talent posted three-year growth 112 points higher and averaged $8M vs. $6M revenue. About one in four employer entrepreneurs has high delegator talent. Averages only, no significance tests.
- **Upwork (Nov 2025, n=2,272 US small-business leaders):** roughly 31% of working time on tasks outside their core expertise, about 77 workdays a year; 73% report burnout. Upwork sells freelance labor; treat as directional.
- **McKinsey (2018):** 37% of time on decisions, 61% of it ineffective.
- Burnout and key-person risk are asserted everywhere but no rigorous owner-specific study quantifies them. Say "practitioner consensus," not "research shows."

## 3. Plain-language classifiers already in circulation

- **Bezos one-way vs. two-way doors (2015 shareholder letter).** Irreversible decisions get slow process; reversible ones should be made fast by individuals or small groups. His warning that organizations apply one-way-door process to two-way-door decisions and slow down is the single most quotable support for pushing leaf and branch decisions down.
- **McKinsey's three types** add frequency to the same idea.

## 4. Attribution and distinctiveness

**Susan Scott, *Fierce Conversations* (2002), Principle 3** has a four-level Decision Tree: leaf (decide and act, no report), branch (decide, act, report on a schedule), trunk (decide, report before acting), root (decided jointly with broad input). Taught by Fierce Inc.; discussed by Human Synergistics and others. Shannon's audience (EOS, peer groups) includes Fierce readers, so the credit line matters. Her existing line is right: inspired by Scott, grown a lot since.

**What is distinctive in Shannon's version:**
1. Five levels. "Limb" separates "talk to me first" from "we decide together," which Scott collapses.
2. Root is the owner's decision with input, not a joint decision. That gives the check a "keep this" message alongside "hand that back." No other framework names the non-delegable layer this cleanly.
3. It's used as a company-wide diagnostic and a living wall, not a one-employee conversation.
4. The psychology of why the owner keeps catching the monkeys is absent from every source above except glancing references in Lowe and Harnish.

**Honest gaps.** No source gives a validated share of owner decisions that "should" be leaf-level, so the check's result is a heuristic, and copy says so by naming a pattern rather than a percentage.

## Implications for the quiz

- Lead with situations and reversibility (two-way doors), which is what the ten scenarios do.
- Use the McKinsey and Gallup numbers sparingly on the landing page if at all; the audience trusts the story more than the stat.
- Keep the Scott credit on the landing page and in the result email.
